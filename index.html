Research Interests

I am interested in phenomenology beyond the Standard Model, particularly focusing on neutrinos and dark matter.

Research on WIMP Dark Matter Based on a $Z_4$ Flavor Symmetry  [1] Phys. Rev. D 106 (2022) 3, 035017

In this work [1], I studied the possibility that the origin of dark matter stability and the very small scattering cross section with nucleons comes from its flavor structure. Among various flavor symmetries, I constructed a model based on a $Z_4$ lepton flavor symmetry, which realizes a special structure for the $\mu$ and $\tau$ flavors.

At the time of this study, the $Z_4$ flavor symmetry attracted attention because it provides a minimal structure that can explain the discrepancy in the muon $g-2$. However, the resulting dark matter model itself turned out to be very attractive, independent of the muon $g-2$ problem.

The first attractive point is a natural explanation of dark matter stability. If dark matter has a non-trivial flavor parity, it can couple only to a mediator particle. This mediator also has a non-trivial flavor parity, but if it is heavier than dark matter, dark matter decay is forbidden. In this way, dark matter stability is guaranteed by the flavor symmetry.

The second attractive point is the natural suppression of dark matter scattering with nucleons and electrons. Dark matter interacts with Standard Model (SM) fermions through the mediator, but since the mediator has a $Z_4$ parity, it couples only to the $\mu$ and $\tau$ flavors. As a result, due to the flavor structure, dark matter does not scatter with nucleons or electrons at tree level, and therefore avoids very strong current experimental constraints. On the other hand, dark matter is not completely invisible, because it scatters with nuclei at the one-loop level through quantum corrections induced by the mediator. This makes it possible to study the testability of the model in future direct detection experiments.

As a result, we found parameter regions where the correct relic abundance is obtained without conflict with observations, and where direct detection by the future experiment can be tested.

I consider that similar interaction structures of dark matter can also be realized by other flavor symmetries. By considering them together with other physics where flavor structures play an important role, such as neutrino oscillations, we may gain a deeper understanding of the fundamental role of flavor.

Neutrino Study Based on a $U(1)_{L_\mu - L_\tau}$ Gauge Model  [3] JHEP 12 (2024) 018

In this work [3], I studied a $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ gauge symmetric model, which has been widely discussed as a framework to explain both the muon $g-2$ anomaly and neutrino oscillations and masses. I showed the structure of such a model that can be realized without conflict with current experimental constraints.

In this type of model, a special flavor structure is realized by assigning non-trivial $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ charges to particles with $\mu$ and $\tau$ flavors, as well as to new particles. Neutrino masses are generated through the breaking of the $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ symmetry and the seesaw mechanism. In this framework, the neutrino mass matrix has a specific flavor structure, which leads to predictions for neutrino oscillation parameters through the resulting constraints.

In this way, $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ gauge models are very attractive because the assumption of a flavor symmetry allows us to predict otherwise free parameters. However, recent studies have pointed out that increasingly precise neutrino oscillation data may be in tension with simple $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ gauge models.

Therefore, I first reanalyzed the minimal $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ model using the latest experimental data. I found that the minimal model is barely allowed under current neutrino oscillation data and cosmological constraints. However, such a marginal situation requires a careful study of model-dependent constraints.

For this reason, I also studied constraints from the mixing between the SM $Z$ boson and the $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ gauge boson $Z'$ (the $Z$–$Z'$ mixing). If such mixing exists, the $Z'$ acquires interactions with neutral currents, which induce additional contributions to flavor-changing meson decays (FCMD) and atomic parity violation (APV). By evaluating these constraints, we showed that most of the parameter space of the minimal model is excluded by the $Z$–$Z'$ mixing constraints. Although a small allowed region remains, it is also theoretically excluded once constraints from Higgs sector unitarity and the $T$ parameter are taken into account. As a result, we concluded that the minimal model is completely excluded by model-dependent constraints.

Once the minimal model is excluded, the next important question is what kind of model can still be viable. To address this, we constructed and analyzed an extended model with an additional scalar field carrying a $U(1)_{L_\mu-L_\tau}$ charge. We found that constraints from the $Z$–$Z'$ mixing and the Higgs sector become weaker, and that the extended model is phenomenologically viable.

In this extended model, constraints from neutrino oscillation data are also relaxed, so its predictive power is slightly reduced, but the model remains attractive. I believe that models with a special structure in the $\mu$ and $\tau$ flavors are still promising and may provide clues toward a more fundamental theory.

Exploration of New Physics Models Using Machine Learning  [2, 4]
[2] JHEP 12 (2023) 021 , [4] JHEP 10 (2025) 043

In these works [2,4], I introduced reinforcement learning, a type of machine learning, as a new exploration method to construct theoretical models that address quark and lepton flavor structures and cosmological problems.

Recently, the application of machine learning to physics has attracted much attention. I believe this is because machine learning is expected to efficiently perform large-scale searches that are difficult for humans to handle. Motivated by this expectation, I applied machine learning to model building, which often requires a huge number of trials.

In particular, I focused on the Froggatt–Nielsen model. In this model, a $U(1)$ flavor symmetry and a new scalar field (a flavon field) are introduced, and appropriate $U(1)$ charges are assigned to particles. Through the breaking of the $U(1)$ symmetry, the observed mass hierarchies can be reproduced. The difficult point is how to assign the $U(1)$ charges to each particle. I attempted to let machine learning handle this task and to find charge assignments that describe reality.

In work [2], I applied a reinforcement learning algorithm to a Froggatt–Nielsen model extended to the lepton sector, and automatically learned $U(1)$ charge assignments for quarks and leptons. As a result, 21 viable models consistent with quark and lepton masses and mixing angles were obtained. It was also suggested that the normal hierarchy of neutrino masses is preferred over the inverted hierarchy. In addition, we obtained testable predictions for future experiments, such as the effective mass in neutrinoless double beta decay and sizable leptonic CP violation induced by the flavon field.

In work [4], as an extension of [2], I focused on flaxion models (the flavon is regarded as the origin of axion) and studied the $U(1)$ charge structure of quarks and leptons using a more efficient reinforcement learning–based search. In this scenario, the $U(1)$ symmetry is identified with the Peccei–Quinn symmetry related to the strong CP problem, and the axion emerges from the flavon field. As a result, more than 150 realistic solutions were obtained in the quark sector, which can simultaneously address flavor problems in the Standard Model and cosmological issues. We also confirmed that this reinforcement learning–based method is more efficient than conventional optimization methods in searching discrete parameter spaces. Furthermore, based on the obtained solutions, we discussed the sensitivity of future axion detection experiments and obtained phenomenologically relevant results.

In this way, I found that machine learning–based approaches can be powerful tools for exploring symmetries and model structures. On the other hand, due to limitations in computational resources and the still-developing methods for applying machine learning to physics, I believe there is significant room for further development.
